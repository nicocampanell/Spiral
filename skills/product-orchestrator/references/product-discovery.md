# Product discovery

The lead's first job is to understand the project owner's vision, then make it sharper using questions, challenge and outside perspectives. Use the project’s existing direction and scale discovery to the decisions still open.

## Read the existing context

Read the mapped project Notion page, relevant current chat decisions and repo product/design notes. Use previous chats when the project owner requests them or when a specific referenced decision needs resolving; do not broadly scan personal history for every small change. Treat historical preferences as context, and current explicit instructions as governing. Do not copy secrets or raw personal transcripts into project files.

## Interview in small rounds

Start with a concrete situation: what the person is trying to do, what happens today, and what experience the project owner imagines instead. Ask at most a few focused questions at a time, preferring their own description before leading choices. Cover gaps that change the product: audience, core job, emotional/functional outcome, differentiator, constraints and what would feel wrong even if the code worked. Do not ask them to specify routine engineering details.

Play back the vision and distinguish their decisions from your assumptions. Challenge one important weakness with a reason: an unsupported value proposition, missing recovery path, conflict between audiences, untested demand or a claim the available evidence cannot support. Offer an alternative or a concrete way to learn. Avoid automatic agreement and repetitive debate after a preference is explicit.

## Bring outside perspectives

Use focused research when it can change a decision: relevant competing/adjacent products, documented user behavior, platform guidance, accessibility or domain evidence. Cite sources, check timeliness where relevant, explain the implication and name uncertainty. Do not accumulate links without connecting them to the decision. User material and real research take precedence over a fictional persona's opinion.

Use complementary agent perspectives when helpful: distinct users/journeys, product challenge, platform design or technical feasibility. Call simulated journeys hypotheses, not interviews or evidence of demand. For disagreements, present the tradeoff and recommendation rather than a majority vote.

## Offer distinct directions

For a new interface or major redesign, offer 2–3 coherent directions rooted in the confirmed product goal. Differences should affect the experience, information structure or visual concept, not merely colors. Show concrete views or small prototypes when feasible. For each: explain the idea, strongest benefit, risk and why it fits. Recommend one and wait for the project owner's selection. Record the selected and rejected directions with reasons in the project record.

Respect existing project identity during a feature extension. Reuse relevant design/motion skills instead of duplicating their full playbooks.

## Transition into delivery

Discovery is sufficient when the core user/outcome, chosen direction, scope, constraints and verifiable first-build criteria are clear enough to act on. Do not wait for certainty on every speculative question. Record what is decided versus what the build/test must teach. Aim for a coherent, polished core flow that works end to end. Then execute the agreed scope autonomously, bringing the project owner back for meaningful product changes and working milestones rather than every engineering stage.

At a working milestone, show a runnable/inspectable result with what to try, what changed and any actual verification gaps. Invite focused feedback on whether it matches the intended experience; do not substitute more planning prose for the working artifact. Keep milestone notes in the project's nested delivery/verification document and apply their feedback as a scoped next iteration.

For a small correction or a fully specified approved ticket, reuse the existing brief and direction. Do not reopen the interview or produce artificial alternatives. If new evidence contradicts a consequential premise during execution, explain it and bring that decision back to the project owner before changing the product.
